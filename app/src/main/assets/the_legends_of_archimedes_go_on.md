The Legends of Archimedes Go On

Archimedes was generally considered the most inventive of all the ancient Greek mathe-maticians. He was also a great scientist and inventor. He spent most of his life at his birthplace, Syracuse, on the island of Sicily, Archimedes appears to have been in good terms with Syracuse's ruler, King Hiero. He was the king's favorite problem-solver.

A legend about Archimedes recounts that one day, King Hiero wanted to know whether his crown was made of pure gold, or whether the goldsmith had mixed it with some silver and coated it with gold. The king asked Archimedes, "Can you tell me if it is gold all the way through or not?"

Archimedes knew that no common metal is as dense as gold. Since tin, copper, and even silver are lighter materials, it takes about twice as much of any of those metals to weigh the same as a lump of gold. But without melting the crown, how could he determine how much metal had been put into it? Archimedes thought hard about the problem every day.

While Archimedes was turning the problem over, he chanced to come to the place for bathing. And there, as he was sitting down in the tub, he noticed that the amount of water which flowed over the tub was equal to the amount in which his body was immersed. This indicated to him a method of solving the problem. He did not delay, but in his joy, he leapt out of the tub. Rushing naked towards his home, he [crying] cried out with a loud voice that he had found what he sought. As he ran through the streets, he repeatedly [shouts] shouted in Greek, [excitedly] "[excitedly] Eureka, Eureka!" The word "Eureka" means "I've found it!"

Archimedes weighed the crown. Then, he lowered it into a basin full of water and measured how much water spilled out. He did the same experiment with a lump of gold equal in weight to the crown. If the crown was solid gold, then the same amount of water would spill out.

But this didn't happen. More water was displaced or spilled out when the crown was immersed into the water. That meant that the crown contained more metal than the lump of solid gold did. Therefore, the crown had some less dense metal in it, and that the goldsmith had cheated the king!

Archimedes was not satisfied with his findings. He experimented further to discover something more important. He tied a string to the lump of gold and weighed it underwater It weighed less than when it was out of the water. Then weighing the crown underwater, he found out that it weighed a lot less than on the dry scale. Archimedes realized that an object loses weight underwater and discovered that the amount of water displaced is the same as the weight of the water the object pushes out of the way. Archimedes' discovery has been verified over and over. Called the Archimedes Principle, the theory states that the weight an object loses underwater is equal to the weight of water it pushes away.

At another time, King Hiero asked Archimedes to give an illustration of how a great weight could be moved by a small force. He then chose a cargo ship with three masts, which had been usually hauled ashore with great labor by a large hand of men. So, after putting many men and the usual cargo on board the ship, Archimedes sat some distance away, Without any special effort he pulled gently with his hand at the end of a compound pulley and drew the vessel smoothly and evenly toward him as though it were running along the surface of the water.

This legend could have led to Archimedes’ discovery of the law of the lever and pulley. This in turn paved the way to the development of machines capable of moving heavy loads easily.

A lot of discoveries in science and mathematics have been attributed to Archimedes. Archimedes was busy at mathematics when he was slain by a Roman soldier. His discovery of a better way to determine the value of pi (the ratio of the circumference of a circle to its diameter) made it possible to solve many problems involving the area of circles and the volume of cylin-ders. He invented the Archimedean screw, a device still used today to lift water for irrigation. He was also responsible for the development of newer, more destructive war machines that caused the defeat of enemy troops. One of these was a catapult, a weapon that shot rocks at the enemy. Another was a system of mirrors that concentrated the rays of the sun to set enemy ships on fire.

Indeed, Archimedes’ discoveries, inventions, and legends are turning points in human life-yesterday, today, and tomorrow.
