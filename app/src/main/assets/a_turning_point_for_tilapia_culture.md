A Turning Point for Tilapia

Many people became interested in cultivating tilapia and one reason for it was the ability of tilapia to multiply rapidly. But this asset soon turned out to be a disadvantage because the fish ponds became overcrowded, thus limiting the growth of the fish. Limited fish growth also meant limited profit. Such was the condition of tilapia culture until Rafael Dineros Guerrero III came along.

Raffy was born in Manila on August 7, 1944. His early interest in nature made him ac-quainted with several plants, animals, and insects.

Early in childhood, he learned to be a responsible person. One of his household chores was to tether and then milk the goats. The family had quite a number of goats which were the usual form of payment that clients gave to his lawyer-father. Another household chore was washing dishes and cleaning the bathroom for his paternal grandmother. Although these tasks were not welcome for a growing boy, the experience served him well when he became a working student at the University of the Philippines. He had earned a scholarship because he graduated with honors from high school. But the scholarship covered only his tuition. His father could send him only a small allowance. So he had to get his expenses for books and other needs from other sources. His first job was washing dishes at the school cafeteria.

He majored in Zoology, and after getting his Bachelor of Science degree, he became a teacher at the University. The following year, he accepted a teaching job at the Mindanao Agriculture College in Musuan, Bukidnon. Upon his arrival, he was promptly assigned to restock the old fishpond which was almost empty and which had broken fish-viewing glass panels. Though be had little knowledge in fisheries, he accepted the challenge. He went to the Bureau of Fisheries now Bureau of Fisheries and Aquatic Resources or BFAR) in Tanay, Rizal and brought back to Musuan carp fingerlings. Then he raised funds for glass panels to replace the broken ones.

Raffy had another challenge which was also related to fisheries. A nearby lake had dried up and Raffy was asked to turn it into a pond. Though Raffy had no knowledge of hydrology. He took the job. He asked some engineer friends to assist him in accomplishing that task.

In 1969, Raffy was able to avail of a scholarship for a Master of Science Degree in Ap-plied Zoology. He graduated the following year. In 1971, he was sent to the United States on a scholarship in fisheries management. For his dissertation, he had the choice of working with temperate fish or with African fish. He chose African fish, specifically tilapia.

While Raffy was working on his dissertation, he saw that the main problem in cultivating tilapia was overpopulation. Tilapia matures early and breeds non-seasonally. As a result, it re-produces early and often, thus overcrowding ponds and limiting further growth. The solution was to control reproduction, but how could it be done? How could the females be differentiated from the males so that they could be separated before they could reproduce?

The most primitive method is by examining their organs but this is too time-consuming and tedious, for the organs are not easy to see. Besides, it can be done only when the fish are about three months old, and by this time, they are almost fully mature and ready to reproduce

. Raffy felt that another method had to be found. While he was studying different researches, he stumbled on the concept of using sex hormones to reverse the sex of fish. It was pioneered by the Japanese scientist, T. K. Yamamoto, who tried it on aquarium fish. However, the technique was not applied commercially.

Raffy decided to apply sex reversal to the blue tilapia. After two years of intense work, he finally succeeded in converting tilapia fry (young fish) to an all male population.

Raffy’s remarkable accomplishment is recognized the whole world over. He has been invited to different countries interested in and dependent on the fruits of fish research. He has inspired many Filipinos to engage in tilapia culture. His research was a major factor in the rapid growth of the tilapia industry. It placed the Philippines on the world map of aquaculture and made it the world leader in tilapia farming.

In 1995, he was awarded the Presidential Lingkod Bayan Award for his outstanding con-tribution to aquaculture technology.

Aside from different projects in aquamarine culture, Raffy also helps tirelessly to restore Manila Bay. His aim is to make it a tourist attraction once more and a source of livelihood and food for city dwellers instead of the dumpsite that it has become.

“We must reach the hearts and minds of people. We must remind them that we are all stewards of the earth and that we should be safeguarding our resources for future generations,” Raffy says with conviction.
