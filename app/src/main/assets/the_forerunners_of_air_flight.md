The Forerunners of Air Flight

The idea of flying in space was first given serious thought in the thirteenth century. It was English monk, Roger Bacon, who thought that perhaps man could fly like birds by using large mificial wings. But it was Leonardo da Vinci, the great Italian artist-scientist, who drew up a plan for a bird machine. His plan envisioned a man flying the machine with his hands flapping the machine’s wings. Of course, it is impossible for a machine like that to fly because a man’s body is very much heavier than a bird’s body.

More serious experiments with flight began in the eighteenth century in France. This time, the experimenters tried to fly with the help of balloons. A balloon filled with hot air goes up in the sky. This is because hot air is lighter than the air in the atmosphere.

Two Frenchmen, the brothers Joseph and Etienne Montgolfier, experimented with silk balloons filled with hot air.

In 1783, they sent up a sheep, a cock, and a duck in a basket hung from the balloon. However, the balloon caught in a trec after an eight-minute flight. The basket broke off but the passengers landed safely. The King of France who watched the experiment was, himself, excited. In fact the experiment caused great excitement all over Europe.

Two months later, Pilatre de Rozier and the Marquis d’Arlanders rose up in the air in a large Montgolfier-type balloon.

They were carried to a distance of about ten kilometers across Paris after which they made a safe descent. They were in the air for twenty-five minutes the first men to fly. Soon people

Were going up in balloons in all parts of Europe. Sometime later, hydrogen was used in balloons instead of hot air. A hydrogen balloon can stay up in the sky for a much longer time. Hydrogen, even when cold, is lighter than air.

Flying in balloons was, however, unsatisfactory, Balloons merely floated with the wind and could not be steered. Chances of accidents were great because balloons could burst, crash, or catch fire in midair. Pilatre de Rozier, in fact, was killed in one such accident.

The idea of steering an airship soon took shape. A French army officer named Meusnier envisioned an airship in the shape of a cigar. He thought of propelling the airship with steam engines which was too heavy and, therefore, impractical.

The first practical airship was invented by Count Ferdinand Zeppelin, a German army of-ficer. Made of aluminum, it had a very large cigar-shaped body, inside which were compartments filled with hydrogen. It had propellers driven by petrol engines. The airship, named Zeppelin, took off with the inventor manning it and flew over Lake Constance in July 1900.

Large Zeppelins which could carry passengers and goods were soon developed. They were used to carry passengers from Germany to North American and Brazil, and back. The Germans also used Zeppelins for dropping bombs over London during World War I.

Airships, however, had many disadvantages. They could not be easily controlled in strong winds because of their shapes and huge sizes. There was an even greater danger from fire be-cause hydrogen catches fire easily. While landing, the German airship. “Hindenburg,” caught fire. This incident left thirty-six casualties.

A number of such accidents brought an end to the use of airships and led to the invention of the airplane which was smaller, faster, and easier to control.
