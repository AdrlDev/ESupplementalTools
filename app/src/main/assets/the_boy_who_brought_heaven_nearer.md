The Boy Who Brought Heaven Nearer

As the lamp swung to and fro, young Galileo noticed that it took as long to swing in a wide arc as in a small are. He timed it with his pulse and found he was right. This was a new thought. He had always supposed that a pendulum would naturally take a longer time to cover the greater distance.

As soon as he got home from church that day, he fixed up a pendulum of his own. He set it going and found that it worked the same way. Then he made more pendulums of different lengths and weights. He hung them from ceiling beams and boughs of trees. His family thought he must be crazy.

These experiments started him on the high road to many other discoveries.

Galileo loved to experiment. He set out to prove things for himself.

In those days, it was thought that if two weights were dropped from a high place, the heav ier would hit the ground first. Galileo questioned it. Then he tried dropping weights from high places. He found it wasn’t true. All weights released together hit the ground at the same time.

One day, he persuaded a group of university professors to go with him to the top of the famous Leaning Tower of Pisa. From there, he dropped a four-kilogram weight and a 500-gram weight together. Both hit the ground exactly at the same moment.

In 1609, Galileo made his greatest discovery. He set up two lenses thirty centimeters apart. He noticed that they made things appear larger. Then, he took larger lenses, put them together, and made the first telescope.

One night and that was a very great night in history he turned his telescopes upon the heavens. The glorious scene startled him. Instead of seeing just a few stars, he saw thousands upon thousands of them. The Milky Way was revealed as a gorgeous band of stars. Suddenly, he realized that the universe was not merely Earth, the sun, the moon, and a few pinpoints of lights called stars. The universe was something infinitely greater and more marvelous. The darkness of space was lit with blazing orbs as far as the human eye could see!

Galileo turned his telescope upon Jupiter and found that the three bright stars on a line with it were not stars at all, but moons, moving around the planet, even as our moon moves around Earth.

Looking at the sun through darkened lenses, he saw a great ball of fire, with dark clouds moving over it that we call sunspots today. He noticed, too, that the sun was moving on its axis, just as Earth does. This was too wonderful. His heart was thrilled. He longed to tell others, and did so. It was all so different from what the church leaders had always believed. They were sure he couldn’t be right. They had him arrested and put in prison.

But Galileo was right, and he knew it. He knew, too, that others would look through the telescopes he had built, and prove that he was right.

As years and centuries rolled by, bigger and bigger telescopes were built. The telescopes revealed that the universe is far greater than even Galileo imagined.
