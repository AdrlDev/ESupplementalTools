Black Beauty

(An excerpt) by Anna Sewell

The first place that I can remember was a large pleasant meadow. At the top of the meadow was a grove of fir trees. At the bottom was a running brook overhung by a steep bank.

At first, when I was very young, I always ran by my mother’s side. But soon, when I wat old enough to eat grass, my mother would go to work in the daytime. I would run with six other colts who lived in the meadow. We had great fun. We used to gallop together round and round the field as fast as we could go.

As I grew older and my coat became shinier, many people came to admire me. I had a fine, black coat, one white foot, and a white star on my forehead.

I was four years old when the farmer said that it was time to train me to be obedient. I had to learn to wear a saddle and a bridle. And I had to carry a person on my back. At first, I hated It. Soon I became accustomed to it. I was trained to pull a carriage. One day in May, I was sold to Squire Gordon. My mother gave me this advice: “Do your best wherever you are. Keep up your good name.”

So I came to Birtwick Pass. I was placed in a large loose box. Two other horses were in stalls next to me. Their names were Merrylegs and Ginger. Soon, I became friendly with both of them.

“What shall we call him?” Squire Gordon asked his wife.

“He’s black as pure ebony and quite a beauty. Let’s call him Black Beauty,” she suggested.

One day, John, the coach driver, harnessed me to the cart. We would take the squire to town. It had been raining heavily and the wind was blowing hard. The water had flooded the road up to my knees in some places. But we got through to the town.

On our way home, we had to cross a low wooden bridge. When I touched the first part of the bridge, I felt sure there was something wrong. I stopped.

“Go on, Black Beauty,” my master urged. But I dared not go forward.

Just then, the keeper of the bridge came running. He was waving a lantern, and shouting. “Hey, stop! The bridge is broken in the middle. Part of it is carried away.”

“ You saved us, Beauty!” said John.That night, Squire Gordon told his wife about me. He said that I had been wiser than both him and John. He said that I saved their lives. Oh, what a good supper John gave me that night!

Then Squire Gordon and his wife decided to visit some friends in town. They were to stay overnight at the hotel. Ginger and I were led to the stables of the hotel. A young man smoking a pipe was asked to fetch some hay.

“ but put out that pipe first,” he was told.

We had settled a little while when I woke up. There was a strange smell in the air. Ginger was coughing and choking. All the horses were stamping in fear. Flames arose from where the forgotten pipe had been left.

James flung open the stable door. He tied a scarf around my eyes. He led me around to safety. The fearless boy rushed back for Ginger. A moment later, the stable roof crashed down.

James was praised for his bravery. When Mrs. Gordon’s brother was looking for a coach-man, James got the job.

We were all sad to see him go. In his place came little Joe Green, the gardener’s boy. He was only fourteen years old. But he was a bright little fellow and always came whistling to his work.

One night, I was suddenly awakened by John. We were to hurry to town to get a doctor, for Mrs. Gordon was very sick.

It was a long, hard ride to town. I galloped like the wind. We hurried across the bridge. Through the dark woods… then uphill… then downhill… until we reached the town.

The town was fast asleep. Only the clatter of my hoofs on the stones could be heard. The church clock struck three as we drew up to Dr. White’s door. John knocked like thunder at the door. A window was opened and Dr. White put his head out.

“Mrs. Gordon is very ill, sir,” said John. “My master asks you to come quickly, please.”

“I will come at once,” said the doctor. “But my horse has been out all day. He is worn out. Can I take your horse?”

The journey back was very hard. I was nearly spent and very hot. But the doctor was a good horseman. He gave me a short rest before the hill. At last, we arrived home where my master was waiting anxiously.

Little Joe Green led me to my stable. But poor young Joe knew very little. He rubbed my legs and chest. But he did not put my warm cloth on. He thought I was so hot I would not like it. Then he gave me a pail of water to drink, and some hay and some corn.

Soon, I began to shake and tremble, and turn cold. I ached all over and oh! How I wished for John.

At last, I heard John at the door. In a moment, he was by my side. He covered me up with three warm cloths. He gave me some hot gruel, and then I think I went to sleep.

I was very ill for several days. John nursed me day and night. My master, too, came often to see me.

“My poor Beauty,” he said one day. “You saved your mistress’ life.” I was very glad to hear that news.

At last, after several days, I woke up one morning feeling much better. However, there was sad news, too. Our mistress was not in very good health. She and the squire had to live in a warmer country. So the big house and the horses were to be sold.

Merrylegs was given to a good home, Joe Green went to look after him. Ginger and I were sold to a country gentleman.We all felt sad at parting.
